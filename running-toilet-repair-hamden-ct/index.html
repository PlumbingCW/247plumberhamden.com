Running Toilet Repair in Hamden, CT: A Homeowner Guide

Find out why your toilet keeps running, what you can check in ten minutes and when the fix is a job for a plumber.

A running toilet is the quiet leak most Hamden households have lived with at some point. Water trickles from the tank into the bowl, the fill valve keeps topping it up, and nothing looks flooded. The only clue is a hiss at night and a water bill that creeps up.

The good news is that most running toilets come down to two or three inexpensive parts. This guide shows you how to find the cause, what a repair looks like and when it is time to bring in our toilet repair team.

How a toilet is supposed to work

The tank holds a measured amount of water. When you flush, the flapper at the bottom lifts, the water drops into the bowl and the flapper falls back to seal the opening. As the tank level falls, the fill valve opens and refills the tank, and a small tube sends a little water to the bowl to restore its trap seal.

Running happens when the flapper does not seal, the fill valve does not shut off or the water level sits too high and spills into the overflow tube. Each has a different fix, so the first job is finding which one you have.

The dye test that takes fifteen minutes

Remove the tank lid and add ten drops of food coloring to the tank water. Do not flush. Wait fifteen minutes and look at the bowl. If color appears, the flapper is leaking into the bowl, which is the most common cause of a running toilet.

If the bowl stays clear but you still hear refilling, look at the water level. When water reaches the top of the overflow tube, the fill valve is not shutting off or the float is set too high. When the level looks normal and the sound continues, the fill valve itself may be worn.

A flapper swap you can do yourself

Close the supply valve behind the toilet, flush to empty the tank and unhook the old flapper from its ears or ring. Take it to a hardware store so you can match the size, since two-inch and three-inch flappers are not interchangeable. Hook the new one on, connect the chain with about half an inch of slack and open the supply again.

Wipe the flapper seat with a cloth while the tank is empty. Mineral scale on the seat is a common reason a new flapper still leaks, and a rough seat that will not clean up is a sign the whole toilet may be wearing out.

When it is the fill valve

Older fill valves use a brass float arm, while newer ones use a plastic cup that rides up a shaft. Replacement kits are inexpensive, and the swap involves closing the supply, draining the tank, unscrewing the supply line and the locknut and installing the new valve at the right height.

If the supply line connector is corroded or the shutoff valve will not close fully, stop. A stuck shutoff valve under a toilet is a poor place to learn how fragile old valves are, and we would rather replace the valve than have it fail.

What a running toilet costs you

A small leak into the bowl can waste many gallons a day without any sound. Water and sewer charges both follow your usage, so a steady leak shows up on the bill twice. Fixing it early is almost always cheaper than ignoring it.

If the meter runs after you have repaired the toilet and closed every other fixture, the leak is somewhere else. Our leak detection page explains how we trace that.

What Hamden's water supply means for toilet parts

The South Central Connecticut Regional Water Authority supplies Hamden and reports that about 88 percent of its water comes from ten reservoirs, with the remaining 12 percent pumped from wells in Cheshire, Hamden, Derby and Seymour. The report does not publish a hardness number, so the authority is the place to ask.

In practice, scale on a flapper seat or fill valve varies from house to house. Check your own tank every year or two, and ask the authority at 203-562-4020 for its annual water quality report if you want the full picture. If you replace the toilet, look for the EPA WaterSense label, which means 1.28 gallons per flush or less. Installation falls under Connecticut's 2022 State Building Code, based on the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC).

When to call a plumber

Call us if the toilet keeps running after a new flapper and fill valve, if water appears on the floor around the base, if the shutoff valve will not turn or if the toilet rocks. Those point to a bigger issue such as a failed seal or a damaged supply line.

We serve North Haven, New Haven and the rest of the area as well as Hamden, and we answer 24/7 if water is actually spilling onto the floor.
